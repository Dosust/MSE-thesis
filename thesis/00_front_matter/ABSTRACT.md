# Abstract

Power and transportation infrastructures are becoming increasingly interdependent through electric-vehicle charging, electrically dependent transportation functions, transportation-supported restoration, and mobile energy resources (Fan et al., 2024; Oladigbolu et al., 2025; Unterluggauer et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2019). Yet structural connection, common exposure, or statistical association does not by itself establish a directional dependency; mechanism, direction, spatial and temporal activation, delay, uncertainty, and validity domain must be qualified explicitly (Ouyang, 2014; Rinaldi et al., 2001). This thesis develops an evidence-grounded mathematical and engineering framework for the **spatiotemporal resilience of coupled Power–Transportation infrastructure**. The system is represented as a **spatiotemporal System of Systems** for architecture and as a **spatiotemporal complex dynamical system** for behavior, while Power and Transportation retain distinct physical laws, topology, service functions, and operating logic; Information and Organization support observation, coordination, and feasible action (Hall et al., 2016; Ibáñez et al., 2010).

The framework connects spatiotemporal structure to evidence-qualified **Power-to-Transportation and Transportation-to-Power interdependencies**, physics-based hybrid multiscale dynamics, state estimation, organizationally feasible control, and uncertainty-aware electricity and mobility service resilience. Directional mechanisms enter the dynamic model only after their mechanism, activation conditions, timing, evidence, uncertainty, and validity domain are qualified. Dynamic coupling is compared with matched independent and static-coupling representations rather than assumed to be superior (Fotouhi et al., 2017; Li et al., 2024; Zou & Chen, 2019). Resilience is evaluated through **viability**, representing whether required services can remain acceptable, and **recovery reachability**, representing whether acceptable service can be restored under feasible actions and uncertainty, with conventional service-recovery measures retained as comparators (Doyen & De Lara, 2010; Poulin & Kane, 2021; Vinod & Oishi, 2021).

Spatiotemporally targeted engineering interventions and infrastructure transformations are evaluated separately in terms of **resilience effect, effectiveness, resource requirements, resource efficiency, and admitted sustainability consequences**. The quantitative sustainability boundary includes direct intervention or recovery cost, operational energy, and qualified carbon-dioxide-equivalent consequences (Mitoulis et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2025). Final decisions are assessed through feasibility, native-unit criteria, Pareto dominance, robustness, and regret before any independently justified preference model is introduced. Verification, validation, uncertainty quantification, provenance, reproducibility, and bounded inference constrain the framework.
