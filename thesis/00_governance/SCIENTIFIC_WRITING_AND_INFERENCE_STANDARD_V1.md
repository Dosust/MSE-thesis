**Status:** CURRENT THESIS-WIDE WRITING / INFERENCE CONTROL  
**Effective date:** 2026-09-17  
**Degree:** Master of Science in Engineering (MSE), Sustainable Engineering  
**Institutional authority:** ASU Graduate College + SSEBE Sustainable Engineering MSE requirements  
**Scientific authorities:** `../../project/RESEARCH_REASONING_PROTOCOL.md`, `../../project/EGMIV_METHOD_V1.md`, `../../project/CANONICAL_RQ_SO_HYPOTHESES_V3.md`  
**Scope:** Chapters I–VII, proposal, appendices, figures, tables, equations, presentations derived from thesis text, and all research claims.

> This is an internal scientific-writing standard. It does not replace the ASU Graduate College Format Manual, the chair-approved style guide, or committee instructions.

---

## 1. Purpose

The thesis must satisfy the SSEBE requirement that thesis research be sufficiently rigorous for publishable-quality work, follow the scientific method, be objective and reproducible, demonstrate independent/original/creative inquiry, and use predefined measurable hypotheses or developmental goals/objectives.

The governing writing chain is

```math
\boxed{
\text{Question}
\rightarrow
\text{Objective}
\rightarrow
\text{Scientific object}
\rightarrow
\text{Assumptions}
\rightarrow
\text{Method}
\rightarrow
\text{Comparator / developmental criterion}
\rightarrow
\text{Evidence}
\rightarrow
\text{Uncertainty}
\rightarrow
\text{Result}
\rightarrow
\text{Inference}
\rightarrow
\text{Validity domain}
\rightarrow
\text{Engineering implication}.
}
```

A broken chain is a thesis defect even if the prose is polished.

---

## 2. Epistemic discipline

Every important statement is controlled during drafting by one primary status:

| Label | Meaning | Required support |
|---|---|---|
| `[E]` | externally established | admissible source + adjacent citation + validity context |
| `[D]` | thesis-derived from stated premises | transparent derivation + cited premises where external |
| `[H]` | hypothesis to be tested | mechanism + comparator + metric + falsification rule |
| `[P]` | proposed thesis construct/method | rationale + test/verification plan |
| `[A]` | explicit assumption | reason + sensitivity/validity consequence where material |
| `[U]` | unresolved/uncertain | reason unresolved + closure plan if thesis-critical |
| `[R]` | result generated by declared experiment | experiment ID + estimate + uncertainty + validity domain |

### Final-prose rule

Mixed labels such as `[D/H]` or `[P/H]` may be useful during brainstorming but are not accepted as final epistemic states. Before thesis prose is frozen, the statement must be decomposed into separate sentences with separate statuses.

```math
\boxed{
\text{literature evidence}
\neq
\text{assumption}
\neq
\text{thesis derivation}
\neq
\text{hypothesis}
\neq
\text{result}.
}
```

## 3. Scientific sentence standard

A scientific sentence must do one identifiable job. It should be one of:

1. **Definition** — states what an object means.
2. **Evidence statement** — reports what an admissible source establishes.
3. **Derivation** — follows logically/mathematically from declared premises.
4. **Assumption** — declares a condition imposed by the thesis.
5. **Hypothesis/proposition** — states a testable claim.
6. **Method statement** — explains what was done and why.
7. **Result statement** — reports an observed/computed outcome.
8. **Interpretation** — explains the meaning of a result within its validity domain.
9. **Limitation** — states what the design cannot establish.
10. **Engineering implication** — states what decision or design consideration changes if the result is valid.

Do not combine several epistemic jobs into one sentence when doing so obscures support.

---

## 4. Claim-to-citation rule

Externally sourced scientific claims require adjacent, verified citations in the chair-approved style; APA 7 author–date is the current internal standard.

Cite the source that supports the **exact claim**, not merely a paper on the same topic.

Adjacent support is required for externally sourced:

- definitions and taxonomies;
- empirical findings;
- theory and methodological statements;
- equations, algorithms and metrics adopted/adapted from literature;
- parameter values, thresholds, datasets and standards;
- claims about literature consensus, limitations, trends or gaps.

Do not add citations to thesis-defined notation, thesis hypotheses, transparent derivations, design decisions or thesis-generated results merely to make them appear established.

### Novelty rule

```math
\boxed{\text{literature support}\neq\text{novelty proof}.}
```

Novelty claims require a separate reproducible search/boundary audit. Absence in a selected bibliography is not proof of absence from the field.

## 5. Operational-definition standard

Before a concept is used as a modeled variable, metric, state, constraint or decision criterion, record:

```math
\boxed{
\text{concept}
\rightarrow
\text{literature meanings}
\rightarrow
\text{adopted thesis definition}
\rightarrow
\text{operational representation}
\rightarrow
\text{unit/domain}
\rightarrow
\text{measurement/model source}
\rightarrow
\text{validity boundary}.
}
```

This applies especially to:

- dependency/interdependency;
- interface;
- service;
- resilience;
- viability;
- reachability;
- effectiveness;
- efficiency;
- sustainability;
- criticality;
- uncertainty;
- transformation.

Definitions must not drift between chapters.

## 6. Mathematical-object standard

Every nontrivial mathematical object must expose, where applicable:

- symbol/name;
- physical or engineering meaning;
- type (scalar/vector/set/function/graph/process/policy);
- index set;
- domain/codomain;
- unit/dimension;
- state/parameter/control/observation status;
- source: established/adapted/derived/proposed;
- assumptions;
- validity domain;
- role in the thesis chain.

An equation is included only if it supports explanation, prediction, control, uncertainty characterization, testing or engineering decision.

```math
\boxed{\text{mathematical sophistication}\neq\text{scientific validity}.}
```

---

## 7. Assumption and validity standard

Every material assumption must answer:

1. Why is the assumption needed?
2. Is it physically plausible?
3. What evidence or benchmark supports it, if any?
4. Which results depend on it?
5. How will sensitivity to it be assessed?
6. What domain becomes invalid if it fails?

Assumed values must never later be described as measured or calibrated values.

The thesis must distinguish:

```math
\boxed{
\text{model applicability domain}
\neq
\text{validation domain}
\neq
\text{generalization domain}.
}
```

---

## 8. Research-question and objective standard

For every research question `RQ_i` there must be one declared objective `SO_i` and a closure path:

```math
\boxed{
RQ_i
\rightarrow
SO_i
\rightarrow
\text{object/method}
\rightarrow
\text{evidence or test}
\rightarrow
\text{result/qualified output}
\rightarrow
\text{Chapter-VII answer}.
}
```

Seven research questions do not require seven hypotheses. Developmental/representational questions may close through predefined verification and evidence criteria rather than artificial directional hypotheses.

---

## 9. Hypothesis standard

Every confirmatory hypothesis must define before execution:

```math
\boxed{
\text{mechanism}
+
\text{treatment/model change}
+
\text{held conditions}
+
\text{baseline/rival}
+
\text{response metric}
+
\text{meaningful-effect criterion}
+
\text{uncertainty}
+
\text{falsification rule}.
}
```

A hypothesis must state what result would fail to support it.

A failed data, evidence, solver or preregistration gate produces **NOT TESTED**, not scientific falsification.

---

## 10. Experiment standard

For confirmatory experiment `i`, use the controlled object

```math
\boxed{
\mathcal T_i=
(H_i,M_i,B_i,\Omega_i,D_i,\theta_i,Z_i,\delta_i,\mathcal F_i,V_i,U_i,C_i,X_i,R_i)
}
```

where, in addition to the existing experiment fields:

- `C_i` = held constants/matched conditions;
- `X_i` = treatment or representation difference actually changed;
- `R_i` = calibration/training/validation/test role of each data object.

Every confirmatory comparison must make explicit what is held fixed and what is changed.

Calibration, verification and validation are separate activities.

---

## 11. Verification, validation and uncertainty

Preserve:

```math
\boxed{\text{verification}\neq\text{validation}.}
```

Verification asks whether the mathematical/computational problem was implemented and solved correctly. Validation asks whether the model is adequate for its intended real-system use.

Minimum verification checks where applicable:

```math
\text{definitions}
\rightarrow
\text{dimensions/units}
\rightarrow
\text{conservation/physics}
\rightarrow
\text{limiting cases}
\rightarrow
\text{numerical convergence}
\rightarrow
\text{regression/unit tests}.
```

Validation must state:

- validation target;
- independent/held-out evidence where available;
- validation metric;
- uncertainty;
- adequacy criterion;
- declared validation domain.

Probability may be used only when a probability model is justified. Otherwise use deterministic, interval, scenario-based or nonprobabilistic robustness representations.

---

## 12. Results-writing standard

Chapter V reports what was obtained, not what the author wishes it to mean.

For each result:

```math
\boxed{
\text{experiment ID}
\rightarrow
\text{estimate}
\rightarrow
\text{uncertainty}
\rightarrow
\text{decision threshold/rule}
\rightarrow
\text{test outcome}.
}
```

Also state, where relevant:

- sample/scenario domain;
- effect size or practical engineering magnitude;
- sensitivity/robustness;
- protocol deviations;
- confirmatory versus exploratory status.

Null and negative outcomes remain reportable results.

Do not convert a numerical difference into a causal statement unless the design supports causal inference.

---

## 13. Discussion-writing standard

Chapter VI must separate interpretation from reporting.

For every major result ask:

```math
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&\text{What mechanism is consistent with the result?}\\
&\text{What rival explanation remains?}\\
&\text{Which assumptions matter most?}\\
&\text{What does uncertainty permit us to conclude?}\\
&\text{How does this compare with prior evidence?}\\
&\text{What engineering decision changes?}\\
&\text{What remains unvalidated?}
\end{aligned}
}
```

Every comparison with external research requires citation. Generalization may not exceed the validation domain.

### Limitation-writing rule

Do not write generic limitations such as “there is uncertainty.” State:

```math
\boxed{
\text{specific assumption/data/model limitation}
\rightarrow
\text{affected quantity}
\rightarrow
\text{possible inference consequence}
\rightarrow
\text{sensitivity/mitigation}.}
```

---

## 14. Conclusion-writing standard

Chapter VII closes the research contract rather than summarizing the document.

For each question:

```math
\boxed{
RQ_i
\rightarrow
\text{result/evidence IDs}
\rightarrow
\text{bounded answer}
\rightarrow
\text{validity domain}
\rightarrow
\text{unresolved remainder}.
}
```

For each hypothesis state one of:

- supported within declared domain;
- not supported within declared domain;
- not tested because prerequisite gate failed;
- exploratory only.

A proposed contribution becomes a supported contribution only when its evidence/test/validation chain closes.

Future work must arise from a stated limitation, unresolved question or validation boundary, not from an unrelated wish list.

## 15. Chapter-specific writing functions

| Chapter | Primary epistemic function | Prohibited substitution |
|---|---|---|
| I | define research contract | results presented as established contribution |
| II | synthesize qualified external evidence | bibliography accumulation |
| III | define/derive scientific and mathematical objects | equations used decoratively |
| IV | specify reproducible tests | post hoc threshold/model selection |
| V | report registered outputs | interpretation inflation |
| VI | interpret within evidence and validity limits | unsupported generalization |
| VII | close RQs/Hs and contributions | new theory or new untested claims |

---

## 16. Writing style

Use professional engineering prose:

- precise nouns and verbs;
- explicit subjects;
- bounded claims;
- consistent terminology and notation;
- short paragraphs organized around one scientific function;
- equations integrated grammatically into the argument;
- figures/tables referenced because they answer a question, not because they are visually attractive.

Avoid:

- “clearly”, “obviously”, “undoubtedly” where evidence should do the work;
- universal claims from limited evidence;
- vague terms such as “better”, “robust”, “efficient”, “significant” without an operational criterion;
- retrospective claims that a method was selected because it performed well;
- novelty language unsupported by the novelty audit.

## 17. Artifact integration

Every central figure, table and equation must have:

```math
\boxed{
\text{question/purpose}
+
\text{provenance}
+
\text{defined symbols}
+
\text{interpretation boundary}
+
\text{chapter/RQ link}.
}
```

A visual simplification may reduce labels but must not alter the underlying scientific semantics.

## 19. Freeze criterion

A thesis section may be labeled scientifically frozen only when:

```math
\boxed{
\text{purpose}
\land
\text{definitions}
\land
\text{evidence}
\land
\text{mathematical consistency}
\land
\text{traceability}
\land
\text{uncertainty/validity}
\land
\text{reproducibility}
}
```

all pass for the declared scope.

Professional wording cannot substitute for a missing scientific gate.
