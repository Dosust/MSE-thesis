## 1.3 Problem Statement

Extreme heat can change the physical state and operating capability of Power-system and Electrified-Transportation components. However, observing thermal stress in both systems does not establish a cross-system interdependency.

The engineering problem is to determine **when a thermal state or temperature-dependent capability in one part of the coupled Power–Electrified Transportation system changes the state, capability, or service of the other part, how that consequence evolves through time, and whether the resulting effect is material for electricity or mobility service**.

Three scientific transitions must therefore be made explicit:

```text
thermal forcing
→ thermal physical state / capability
→ qualified cross-system thermal mechanism
```

```text
qualified thermal mechanism
→ state-time propagation
```

```text
state-time propagation
→ electricity / mobility service consequence
```

The thesis does not treat structural connection, common exposure, temperature correlation, or model coupling as sufficient evidence of thermal interdependency. Each active pathway must have a physically interpretable direction, thermally conditioned mechanism, state consequence, temporal consequence, and declared validity boundary.

The resulting problem is deliberately narrow:

```text
Extreme heat
→ thermal interdependency
→ state
→ time
→ service consequence
```
