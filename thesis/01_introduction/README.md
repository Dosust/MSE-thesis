## Canonical state-time interpretation

> **State in this thesis refers to the minimum set of evolving physical or operational variables required to characterize the current condition of the Power System and Electrified Transportation System and to determine their subsequent evolution under extreme-heat forcing. The primary states of interest are thermal states; non-thermal states are included only where required to represent the thermal interdependency or its service consequence.**

> **Time in this thesis represents the temporal evolution of those states and the timing of critical thermal transitions, interdependency propagation, service degradation, and, where modeled, service recovery.**

Preserve:

```text
external forcing ≠ system state
physical quantity ≠ state variable ≠ active thesis state
thermal state ≠ operational/energy state
state ≠ capability ≠ service
transition time ≠ propagation delay ≠ service-consequence time ≠ recovery time
```

Exact active states remain OPEN and must satisfy minimum-sufficient physics.

## Canonical resilience interpretation

> **Resilience is the ability of the coupled Power and Electrified Transportation systems to maintain or recover required electricity and mobility services as thermal states and thermal interdependencies evolve over time.**

Chapter I must treat resilience as a service-level state-time concept, not component survival. Preserve:

```text
Thermal State ≠ Capability ≠ Service ≠ Resilience
```

Improve, Adapt, and Restore are engineering response classes; they are not the definition of resilience.

## Canonical sustainability interpretation

> **Sustainability in this thesis is assessed through two pathways: (1) the direct environmental, economic, and social consequences of thermal interdependencies on electricity and mobility services, and (2) the environmental, economic, and social consequences of engineering responses used to improve, adapt, or restore those services. Both pathways are evaluated within a declared system boundary and time horizon.**

Chapter I must present **one sustainability framework with two pathways**:

```text
Pathway 1
Thermal Interdependency
→ Electricity / Mobility Service Consequence
→ Environmental / Economic / Social Consequence

Pathway 2
Engineering Response
→ Changed State / Capability / Service
→ Environmental / Economic / Social Consequence
```

Dimension families:

```text
Environmental → energy use; energy efficiency; electrical/conversion losses; GHG emissions; material/resource burden
Economic → operating; maintenance/replacement; intervention; service-disruption cost
Social → access; continuity; affordability; equity; safety
```

SDG linkage is an interpretation layer only:

```text
Measured Sustainability Indicator
→ Sustainability Dimension
→ Relevant SDG Target / Indicator
```

Exact indicators, functional unit, accounting boundary, time horizon, and SDG targets remain OPEN.

## Canonical effectiveness and energy-efficiency placement

```text
Effectiveness → Resilience / Engineering-Response Assessment
Energy Efficiency → Environmental Sustainability
```

Effectiveness evaluates whether an engineering response maintains or recovers required service. Energy efficiency evaluates useful service relative to energy input and is treated as an environmental-sustainability indicator when measurable.

## Permanent distinctions

```text
common heat exposure ≠ thermal interdependency
connection ≠ directional dependency
thermal state ≠ capability ≠ service ≠ resilience ≠ sustainability
effectiveness ≠ energy efficiency ≠ sustainability
effective resilience response ≠ sustainable response
transition time ≠ propagation delay ≠ recovery time
simulation ≠ validation
```

The active Chapter I must not reintroduce generic Information/Organization, viability/reachability, broad sustainability optimization, or multiobjective engineering-decision analysis as primary research objects. Sustainability remains bounded to the canonical two-pathway assessment.
