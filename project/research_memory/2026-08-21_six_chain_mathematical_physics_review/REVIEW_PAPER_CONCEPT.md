# Review Paper Concept

## Working title

**A Mathematical–Physical Ontology of Sustainable and Resilient Infrastructure Systems: From Governing Laws to Societal Learning**

Alternative thesis-aligned title:

**Mathematical Physics of Sustainable Infrastructure Resilience: A Layered Ontology of Physical Dynamics, Inference, Failure, Viability, Engineering Action, and Societal Decision**

## Core research question

> Where, exactly, do mathematics and physics enter the complete science of sustainable and resilient infrastructure systems?

## Main contribution

The paper should not claim that the literature already defines exactly six chains. Instead:

> The literature contains recurrent but fragmented scientific layers. Through systematic synthesis, these layers can be organized into six interacting analytical chains spanning physical laws, knowledge generation, disturbance propagation, viability and recovery, engineering lifecycle intervention, and societal decision-learning.

## Review objectives

1. Identify all recurrent layers used to study sustainable/resilient infrastructure systems.
2. Define the core scientific question of each layer.
3. Map each layer to the mathematics and physics used there.
4. Identify canonical equations and model classes.
5. Map representative literature anchors to layers.
6. Separate physical-state science, epistemic/inference science, resilience science, engineering intervention, and normative societal evaluation.
7. Identify missing links between layers.
8. Identify where existing literature treats interfaces statically or without identifiability.
9. Identify where service-to-population/equity mappings are absent.
10. Build an integrated ontology that is reproducible and falsifiable.

## Proposed paper structure

1. Introduction: fragmentation problem
2. Review protocol and coding ontology
3. Chain I — governing physics, topology, interfaces and dynamics
4. Chain II — sensing, inverse inference, estimation, UQ and prediction
5. Chain III — hazards, fragility, cascades, functionality, services and consequences
6. Chain IV — viability, reachability, resilience, recovery and control
7. Chain V — design, operation, aging, maintenance, planning and adaptation
8. Chain VI — sustainability, equity, economics, governance and decision-learning
9. Cross-cutting mathematical engines
10. Cross-chain couplings and feedbacks
11. Gaps and thesis research opportunities
12. Proposed unified mathematical architecture
13. Limitations
14. Conclusions
