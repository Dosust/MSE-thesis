# Two-Minute Sustainability Video — Preserved Content

## Intended topic

History, evolution, and present-day dimensions of sustainability.

## Core narrative

Today, sustainability is much more than protecting the environment. It has evolved through decades of scientific, economic, and social thinking into an integrated framework for our future.

Originally, sustainability focused mainly on conserving natural resources. Industrialization brought unprecedented economic growth alongside increasing resource consumption, pollution, and environmental degradation. By 1972, *The Limits to Growth* used systems modelling to question whether continuous economic and population growth could continue on a planet with finite resources.

A major turning point came in 1987 with the United Nations Brundtland Report, *Our Common Future*. Sustainable development was defined as development that meets present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.

During the following decades, sustainability increasingly developed around three interconnected dimensions: environmental, economic, and social sustainability.

Environmental sustainability addresses climate change, energy, water, biodiversity, natural resources, pollution, waste, and ecosystem protection.

Economic sustainability focuses on long-term economic viability, including costs, productivity, investment, resource efficiency, innovation, life-cycle costs, and sustainable value creation.

Social sustainability focuses on people: health, safety, equity, human rights, employment, working conditions, inclusion, accessibility, quality of life, and community well-being.

In 2015, the United Nations adopted the 17 Sustainable Development Goals, expanding sustainability into a global framework connecting environmental protection, economic development, and human well-being. Scientific frameworks such as planetary boundaries have also strengthened understanding of environmental limits within which societies and economies operate.

Today, sustainability is increasingly connected with circularity, resilience, climate action, responsible governance, technological innovation, and equity.

Its evolution can therefore be represented as:

`Natural-resource conservation -> Environmental protection -> Sustainable development -> Environment + Economy + Society -> SDGs + Climate action -> Circularity + Resilience + Equity -> Integrated sustainability today`

Closing question:

> How can we design societies and systems that are environmentally responsible, economically viable, socially equitable, and resilient over the long term?

## Important accuracy note

Do not claim that the Brundtland Report itself formally introduced the modern "three pillars" as a named three-pillar framework. It established the influential sustainable-development definition and strongly linked development, social needs, economic development, and environmental protection; the three-pillar representation developed through subsequent sustainability literature and policy practice.
